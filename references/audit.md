# Long-Form Audit

Use this reference for substantial writing after the main draft is complete.

Do not run this audit mechanically on short messages or simple rewrites. Its purpose is to catch problems that only become visible across several paragraphs or sections.

Read `patterns.md` before using this file. Use `genres.md` when the document belongs to a recognizable genre.

## What this audit is for

Sentence-level editing can miss patterns created by the document as a whole.

A phrase may sound fine once and become noticeable after appearing six times.

Two sections may each work on their own while following exactly the same structure.

A conclusion may repeat an idea that was already stated in the introduction and several section endings.

This audit looks for those document-level problems.

Do not report the audit unless the user asks for findings.

Fix the text silently when the request is for finished writing.

---

## Audit order

Review in this order:

1. factual and source fidelity
2. information progression
3. repetition across distance
4. document architecture
5. paragraph construction
6. sentence rhythm
7. rhetoric and abstraction
8. evidence and certainty
9. openings and endings
10. surface language

Fix higher-level problems before polishing individual words.

A weak paragraph should not receive a vocabulary polish before deciding whether the paragraph belongs at all.

---

## Pass 1: source fidelity

Compare the draft against the supplied material.

Check every concrete claim that could have changed during rewriting.

Look especially for:

- numbers
- dates
- names
- causal claims
- quoted language
- technical distinctions
- levels of certainty
- scope
- attribution

Remove details introduced only to make the prose more vivid or specific.

Do not upgrade:

> may

to:

> will

Do not upgrade:

> contributed to

to:

> caused

Do not turn a possibility into a general rule.

If the source expresses uncertainty, preserve it where the uncertainty matters.

Check whether examples still represent the original claim accurately.

A cleaner sentence is not an improvement if it changes the evidence.

---

## Pass 2: information progression

Read each paragraph and identify what new information it contributes.

If two consecutive sentences perform the same job, combine them or remove one.

If a paragraph merely restates the previous paragraph at a higher level, decide whether it is needed.

Watch for sequences such as:

> X happens.

> This means X can happen.

> This is important because X matters.

Usually only one of those sentences contains useful information.

Each paragraph should leave the reader somewhere different from where it began.

That change may be:

- a new fact
- a mechanism
- a consequence
- a qualification
- an example
- a narrower interpretation
- a connection to another part of the argument

Do not force every paragraph to contain one of each.

Check whether the document repeatedly pauses to explain what the reader should conclude instead of continuing the analysis.

Delete sentences that only announce significance.

---

## Pass 3: distant repetition

Look beyond adjacent sentences.

Search mentally across the entire document for ideas that keep returning in slightly different language.

Common examples:

- "the task remains unresolved"
- "the context has changed"
- "the model gets another turn"
- "runtime controls are different"
- "the final action does not explain the run"

Some repetition may be necessary. Technical arguments often depend on recurring concepts.

The problem is repeated explanation.

Ask:

> Is this occurrence adding something, or reminding the reader of something already established?

If it only reminds, shorten it or remove it.

Do not use synonym rotation to disguise repetition.

If the document has already established "runtime control," changing later references to "execution safeguard," "system-level enforcement," and "external control mechanism" usually makes the prose worse.

Keep the stable term and remove unnecessary restatement.

Also check repeated examples.

If three sections all use a failed command as the example, decide whether each use is doing different work.

## Pass 4: document architecture

Look at the section structure without reading sentence by sentence.

Check whether the document has become too orderly.

Warning signs include:

- every section having similar length
- every section containing the same number of paragraphs
- headings using identical grammatical forms
- every section opening with a definition
- every section ending with a takeaway
- repeated "problem → explanation → lesson" structure
- repeated "X is useful, but X can also be dangerous" structure

Do not introduce asymmetry for its own sake.

Instead, let the material determine the section shape.

A simple point may need two paragraphs.

A difficult mechanism may need six.

Some sections may need examples. Others may not.

Some headings may be descriptive while others name a concrete question or mechanism.

Remove sections that exist mainly to make the outline look complete.

Merge neighboring sections when they are explaining the same mechanism from slightly different angles.

Split a section when it contains two arguments that need different treatment.

---

## Pass 5: paragraph structure

Check whether paragraphs repeatedly use the same internal pattern.

For example:

> claim  
> explanation  
> example  
> conclusion

That structure is fine occasionally. It becomes visible when every paragraph uses it.

Watch for repeated openings such as:

> This happens because...

> This matters because...

> This becomes important when...

> The reason is...

Vary the way information enters the paragraph because the reasoning varies, not because variety itself is the goal.

Check paragraph length.

A long document made entirely of similarly sized paragraphs can feel generated even when the sentences are good.

Allow short paragraphs when a point is complete.

Allow longer paragraphs when several sentences belong to the same line of reasoning.

Do not split a paragraph merely to create visual rhythm.

Do not merge unrelated material merely to avoid short paragraphs.

---

## Pass 6: sentence rhythm

Read several paragraphs at a time.

Listen for repeated sentence shapes.

Watch especially for:

- three short sentences in sequence
- repeated two-clause contrasts
- repeated "X can..., Y can..." constructions
- repeated sentences beginning with "This"
- repeated sentences beginning with "If"
- repeated colon constructions
- repeated final short sentence
- repeated clause lengths

One instance may sound natural. Repetition makes the pattern visible.

Do not fix rhythm by randomly changing punctuation.

Change the underlying syntax.

If several sentences contain parallel claims, consider combining the ideas around their causal relationship.

Instead of:

> The tools stayed the same. The task stayed the same. The context changed.

Prefer:

> The model was working from a changed context even though the tools and task were unchanged.

If the contrast itself feels too polished, restructure again around the mechanism.

Do not chase maximum sentence variety.

Readable prose naturally repeats some structures.

The goal is to remove conspicuous recurrence.

---

## Pass 7: rhetoric and abstraction

Look for places where the draft sounds more important than the source material warrants.

Flag language that turns an ordinary mechanism into a broad principle.

Examples:

> This changes everything.

> This gets to the heart of the problem.

> The implications are profound.

> This is a fundamental shift.

Replace these only when the source supports a concrete consequence.

Otherwise remove them.

Check abstract noun density.

A sentence containing several terms such as:

> persistence, optimization, alignment, execution, authority, context

may still be correct, but ask whether the sentence explains how those things interact.

Prefer the mechanism.

Avoid creating new labels for ideas used once.

Do not call something:

> execution persistence drift

when the paragraph can simply explain that the task remains open after a failed action.

Review metaphorical language.

Keep metaphors that genuinely improve understanding.

Remove metaphors that mainly make the prose memorable.

---

## Pass 8: evidence and certainty

Check claims that sound general.

Words that deserve attention include:

- always
- never
- inevitably
- typically
- usually
- often
- commonly
- widely
- fundamentally

These may be justified, but the source should support the scope.

Check phrases such as:

> research shows

> experts agree

> studies have found

> it is well known

If the source does not identify the evidence, remove or narrow the claim.

For named research, preserve what the source actually found.

Do not extend a result from an evaluation environment to deployed systems without saying that the environments differ.

Do not treat one incident as evidence for prevalence.

Do not insert caveats everywhere.

Add uncertainty only where the evidence requires it.

Too much defensive qualification can become its own repetitive pattern.

---

## Pass 9: openings, transitions, and endings

### Section openings

Check whether the heading and first sentence say the same thing.

Heading:

> ## Why context changes matter

Weak opening:

> Context changes matter because the model generates from the context it receives.

Better:

> A failed tool call can change what the model receives on the next invocation.

Start the section by moving into the substance.

### Transitions

Remove transitions that only announce movement.

Examples:

> With that in mind...

> This brings us to...

> Turning now to...

> Against this backdrop...

If the relationship is obvious, begin the next point.

Keep transitions when the reader genuinely needs help understanding the connection.

### Section endings

Check whether every section closes with a polished takeaway.

Common generated endings include:

> That is the real risk.

> And that is where the problem begins.

> The boundary has to live outside the model.

> The lesson is simple.

These lines may sound effective in isolation and repetitive across a document.

End a section when its argument is complete.

The final sentence can be technical, qualified, descriptive, or short. It does not need to sound quotable.

### Document ending

Read the last two sections together.

Remove conclusions that repeat points already made clearly.

Do not summarize every section unless the genre calls for a summary.

A strong ending often leaves the reader with the last necessary implication rather than a compressed version of the entire document.

## Pass 10: surface cleanup

Run this pass only after structural editing.

Check for repeated model-favored words.

Possible examples include:

- crucial
- landscape
- leverage
- robust
- seamless
- nuanced
- underscore
- highlight
- foster
- navigate
- realm
- transformative
- pivotal

Do not ban a word merely because models use it often.

Replace it when a plainer word fits better.

Check for inflated verbs:

> utilize → use

> facilitate → help, allow, or enable when accurate

> serves to demonstrate → shows

> possesses → has

Do not perform blind substitutions.

Check punctuation density.

Reduce em dashes when they repeatedly create dramatic pivots.

Check bolding.

Ordinary prose rarely needs multiple emphasized phrases in every section.

Check headings.

Remove headings that simply turn every few paragraphs into a named concept.

Check lists.

Convert a list to prose when its items are not genuinely separate.

Keep the list when scanability matters.

---

## Do not overcorrect

The audit should not produce sterile prose.

Do not remove all:

- contrast
- repetition
- rhythm
- short sentences
- long sentences
- lists
- rhetorical devices
- technical terminology
- section structure

Natural writing uses all of them.

The problem is mechanical recurrence or structure that contributes little beyond polish.

Do not rewrite a clear sentence merely because it matches a known pattern.

Check whether the construction is doing useful work in its local context.

Do not force irregularity.

Deliberately inserting awkwardness, fragments, slang, or inconsistent punctuation does not make prose more human.

Do not make factual writing vague in the name of naturalness.

Do not remove legitimate precision.

---

## Final acceptance test

Before returning a substantial document, read it once without editing sentence by sentence.

Ask:

- Does each section have a reason to exist?
- Does the argument move forward rather than circle around the same point?
- Are any claims repeated mainly for emphasis?
- Do several sections follow suspiciously similar shapes?
- Does the prose keep falling into the same cadence?
- Are technical terms stable?
- Is any sentence more dramatic than the evidence?
- Has the rewrite added unsupported certainty?
- Are headings doing useful organizational work?
- Do section endings sound written for the argument rather than for effect?
- Did editing flatten the writer's voice?
- Does the final paragraph actually need to be there?

Then inspect the passages that still feel unusually polished.

Check them against `patterns.md`.

If the document belongs to a specific genre, make one final pass with `genres.md`.

If a difficult passage remains ambiguous, consult `examples.md`.

Stop when the writing is clear, faithful, and natural. Do not keep rewriting simply to make the text less predictable.
