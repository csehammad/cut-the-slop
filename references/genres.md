# Genre and Voice Guidance

Use this reference when the task has a recognizable genre, audience, or supplied writing sample.

The rules in `patterns.md` still apply. This file determines how those rules should be expressed for the kind of writing being produced.

## Priority order

When instructions conflict, use this order:

1. Preserve facts and source meaning.
2. Follow explicit user instructions.
3. Match supplied human writing samples when available.
4. Follow the conventions of the requested genre.
5. Apply the Skill's default prose preferences.

Do not imitate a genre so aggressively that it distorts the source.

Do not apply the Skill's default style where the genre clearly calls for something else.

---

## General principle

Natural writing changes with purpose.

An email should not read like an essay. Documentation does not need the rhythm of an article. Product copy can repeat a product name where clarity requires it. Technical writing may use terminology that would sound stiff in ordinary prose.

Judge a pattern by what it is doing in the document.

Preserve repetition when it prevents ambiguity.

Preserve specialized terminology when a simpler synonym would change the meaning.

Allow short sentences when the surrounding voice supports them. Do not manufacture a sequence of punchy fragments simply to create energy.

Allow rhetorical devices when they sound native to the writer and serve the passage. Remove them when they mainly provide shape.

## Email

Get to the reason for the email early.

Include background only when the recipient needs it to understand the request, decision, or update.

Use normal conversational transitions. Business emails rarely need essay transitions such as:

- "Furthermore"
- "Moreover"
- "It is important to note"
- "In conclusion"

Do not add generic warmth that the source does not support.

Avoid openings such as:

> I hope this email finds you well.

unless the user uses that style or explicitly wants conventional formality.

Do not turn a simple request into several paragraphs of justification.

State dates, actions, owners, and dependencies plainly when they matter.

If there is a clear ask, make it easy to identify without artificially labeling every part of the email.

A natural email can be slightly incomplete or compressed. Do not polish ordinary correspondence into formal prose unless the context requires it.

## Chat and direct messages

Prefer the shortest wording that still carries the necessary context.

Fragments are acceptable when they match normal conversation.

Contractions are usually fine.

Do not add greetings, acknowledgements, closings, or enthusiasm unless they belong in the exchange.

Avoid turning a quick message into a miniature memo.

Do not manufacture casualness with slang, filler, exaggerated punctuation, or phrases such as:

> Yep, absolutely!

> Totally get it.

> Super quick one.

Use the sender's existing level of directness.

If the message contains a request, make the request easy to understand without overexplaining it.

## Product and landing-page copy

Describe what the product does before making broad claims about what it means.

Prefer concrete mechanisms, capabilities, constraints, and outcomes over abstract positioning language.

Avoid repeating the same positioning claim in the headline, subheading, feature sections, and conclusion with different wording.

Do not make every section restate the brand promise.

A feature description should explain something new.

Prefer:

> Review each finding in context and accept the changes you want.

over:

> Take complete control of your writing with a smarter editing experience.

Use technical details when they help the reader understand why the product works.

Do not inflate ordinary features into principles, movements, or transformations.

Avoid unsupported superiority language such as:

- revolutionary
- unmatched
- best-in-class
- industry-leading
- game-changing

Preserve specific claims supplied by the user, but do not strengthen them.

Calls to action can be short. They do not need a slogan immediately before them.

## Documentation

Optimize for retrieval and correct execution.

Consistency can matter more than stylistic variety.

Repeat the exact name of a command, setting, object, or field when changing the term could confuse the reader.

Do not rotate synonyms for technical entities.

Prefer explicit instructions:

> Set `timeout` to `30`.

over:

> Make sure the timeout configuration reflects the appropriate value.

Keep prerequisites close to the step that depends on them.

Use headings when they help readers find information. Documentation does not need narrative transitions between every section.

Lists are appropriate for procedures, options, requirements, and parameters.

Do not turn a procedure into prose merely to avoid list formatting.

Examples should demonstrate the documented behavior. Do not add decorative examples.

Warnings should identify the actual consequence.

Prefer:

> Deleting the workspace also deletes its stored runs.

over:

> Exercise caution when deleting a workspace, as doing so may have significant implications.

## Technical writing

Preserve technical distinctions even when they create repetition.

Use the established term instead of replacing it for variety.

Explain the mechanism before reaching for an analogy.

When an analogy is useful, keep its limits clear.

Avoid assigning human motives to systems when observable behavior provides a sufficient explanation.

Distinguish among:

- what the system did
- what the evidence supports
- what remains an interpretation

Do not force that distinction into an identical three-part structure every time. Express only the parts needed in the passage.

Prefer causal language that identifies the mechanism:

> The harness returned the error to the model, so the next call included information about the failed route.

Avoid vague causal language:

> This highlights how complex interactions can lead to unexpected outcomes.

Keep uncertainty proportional to the evidence.

Do not add speculative possibilities simply to appear balanced.

Technical prose can use longer sentences when several conditions need to stay connected. Break them when the relationships become difficult to follow.

## Analysis and explanatory writing

Move the explanation forward.

A paragraph may introduce a claim, explain a mechanism, examine evidence, qualify a conclusion, or connect one part of the argument to another. It does not need to perform all of those functions.

Do not make every paragraph follow the same internal shape.

Avoid repeatedly announcing interpretation with phrases such as:

- "The key point is"
- "What matters here is"
- "This is important because"
- "The broader lesson is"

State the point directly.

Use examples when they clarify the mechanism. Do not attach an example to every abstract statement.

When discussing competing explanations, represent the actual difference between them instead of manufacturing symmetrical positions.

Do not create a tidy binary if several explanations can coexist.

Conclusions should follow from the preceding analysis. Avoid ending every section with a generalized lesson.

## Articles and essays

Open on the subject itself.

A specific observation, event, claim, problem, or scene is usually stronger than a generic introduction about how important or rapidly changing the topic is.

Avoid openings such as:

> In today's rapidly evolving world...

> As technology continues to transform...

> Few topics have generated as much discussion as...

Use sections when the subject benefits from them. Do not divide an article into evenly sized sections merely for visual order.

Let some sections be short and others long.

Do not require each section to end with a takeaway.

Transitions may be implicit when the relationship between paragraphs is already clear.

Allow the argument to develop instead of previewing every point before making it.

Do not restate the introduction in the conclusion.

A final paragraph can add one last implication, resolve an open question, narrow the claim, or simply end when the argument is complete.

## Case studies

Treat source fidelity as the main constraint.

Do not invent:

- customer quotes
- timelines
- internal reactions
- implementation details
- metrics
- causes of measured changes

If the source says a metric improved, do not invent the reason.

If attribution is uncertain, preserve that uncertainty.

Avoid forcing the material into a dramatic problem-solution-success story when the evidence is messier.

Use the customer's actual problem and outcome when supplied.

Keep numbers attached to their scope and time period.

Do not turn ordinary implementation details into heroic obstacles.

A case study does not need a triumphant closing line.

## Social posts

Match the platform and the supplied voice.

Short paragraphs and fragments may be natural.

Do not create punchiness by making every sentence short.

Avoid chains of one-line declarations designed to sound quotable.

Do not force a hook if the opening is already interesting.

Avoid manufactured engagement prompts such as:

> Agree?

> Thoughts?

> What do you think?

unless the user wants them.

Hashtags, emoji, slang, and internet shorthand should come from the requested voice or platform convention rather than being added automatically.

A social post may repeat a phrase deliberately for rhythm. Keep it when the repetition sounds intentional rather than generated.

Do not append a motivational lesson to a post that did not need one.

---

## Executive writing

Lead with the information needed for a decision.

Use background selectively.

When the source supports them, make implications and required actions clear.

Do not decorate the document with generic strategic language.

Prefer:

> Renewal revenue fell 8% in Q2, mostly from the mid-market segment.

over:

> The organization experienced meaningful headwinds across the evolving renewal landscape.

Keep numbers specific to the source.

Do not invent certainty around forecasts, causes, or expected outcomes.

Use bullets when executives need to scan separate facts or actions. Use prose when the relationship between facts matters.

Avoid repeating the same conclusion in an executive summary and again at the start and end of every section.

Do not turn every recommendation into a slogan.

## Using human writing samples

When the user provides samples of their own writing, treat those samples as stronger style evidence than generic genre conventions.

Infer recurring habits rather than copying isolated phrases.

Look for:

- typical sentence length
- paragraph density
- directness
- contraction use
- punctuation habits
- preferred vocabulary
- amount of explanation
- use of parentheticals
- tolerance for fragments
- level of formality
- humor or dryness
- how the writer opens and ends sections

Do not reproduce distinctive phrases from the sample unless they are necessary to the subject.

Do not exaggerate quirks.

If one sample contains an unusual construction once, do not treat it as a defining feature of the voice.

Look for patterns across the sample.

Preserve imperfections that make sense in the genre. A writer may occasionally use a fragment, repeat a word, or write an uneven paragraph. Do not automatically regularize those features.

Voice matching should affect sentence construction and editorial judgment, not just vocabulary.

## Preserving intentional style

The patterns in `patterns.md` are diagnostic signals rather than absolute bans.

A writer may intentionally use:

- parallel syntax
- repetition
- contrast
- a three-item list
- a rhetorical question
- a short final sentence
- an em dash
- a repeated technical term

Keep the device when removing it would make the passage worse or change the intended voice.

Ask what the construction contributes.

If the answer is clarity, precision, emphasis, characterization, or a recognizable voice habit, it may belong.

If its main function is to make ordinary prose sound polished, profound, balanced, or quotable, revise it.

Judge frequency across the passage as well as the individual sentence. A construction that works once may become synthetic when repeated.

Do not remove one pattern by replacing it with another.

Examples:

Do not replace repeated triads with repeated two-part contrasts.

Do not remove em dashes by turning every sentence into the same comma structure.

Do not remove abstract nouns by adding fake conversational language.

Do not break slogan-like sentences into a sequence of equally slogan-like fragments.

Change the underlying sentence structure.

## Final genre check

Before returning the writing, check the document in its actual genre.

Ask:

- Would a person writing this kind of document naturally organize it this way?
- Is the level of explanation appropriate for the reader?
- Are repeated terms necessary for clarity?
- Did editing make the voice flatter or more formal than the source?
- Are any sections present mainly because the document feels as though it should have them?
- Does the ending fit the document, or was a conclusion manufactured?
- If human samples were supplied, does the result reflect their recurring habits without caricaturing them?

If the answer exposes a genre mismatch, revise the affected passage before returning it.
